a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30   42) \begin{abstract}
85f768a3 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13   43) This thesis asks whether formal protest petitions against housing development proposals in Austin, Texas can be ranked by risk before formal public hearings, using only information available at the time of filing. The outcome is a \emph{measured threshold-crossing petition}---an operationalized indicator of whether the reconstructed petition-share calculation exceeded the statutory 20\% threshold in public records. Because municipal clerk certification is not directly observed, this measure is distinct from the clerk-certified valid petition (unobserved).

a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30   70) \section{Introduction}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30   71) 
7d8582f2 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-12   72) As the United States confronts a persistent housing affordability crisis, understanding local barriers to new housing supply remains an important empirical challenge. Traditional accounts emphasize the role of municipal zoning laws, but a substantial share of delay and blockage occurs through discretionary political processes---public hearings, protest petitions, and council negotiations---that collectively slow or block individual projects. This thesis uses Austin, Texas as a case study. Between 2010 and 2024, rapid employment growth drove severe housing supply constraints, inflating property values and generating sustained conflict between neighborhood preservation and regional development goals. The study is historically bounded to Austin's pre-HB\,24 protest petition regime (2007--2024) and serves as a template for analyzing institutional change in local land-use governance, not as a near-term operational tool under current Texas law.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30   73) 
7d8582f2 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-12   74) Against this backdrop, the central research question is whether a measured threshold-crossing petition---defined below in Section~\ref{sec:outcome}---can be predicted before formal public hearings begin.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30   75) 
53bf5b30 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13   76) The outcome under study is specific. Under the pre-2025 framework of Texas Local Government Code \S~211.006 \cite{texas211006}, adjacent property owners may file a protest petition; if signatures representing 20\% of the relevant adjoining land area were collected and the municipal clerk certified the petition as valid, the petition historically required a three-fourths supermajority for City Council approval. (Effective September 1, 2025, House Bill 24 repealed and replaced this mechanism with a differentiated, multi-tier system under \S~211.0061 \cite{hb24_2025,texas2110061}.) This is one institutionalized form of neighborhood opposition---the most consequential and best-observed form in the Austin regulatory context---not a prediction of opposition in all its forms. The thesis narrows its claim accordingly: the primary contribution is an assessment of whether administrative, spatial, and economic features observable at the time of a zoning application are sufficient to anticipate this specific institutional event.
e7f5ad18 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05   77) 
13c51205 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13   78) To answer this question, the research uses a staged workflow but limits headline inference to Stage~C. The chapter flow is intentionally compact: Introduction; outcome definition and as-of information setup; Stage~C primary results; limitations; and brief references to supporting analyses in appendices.

84859ac0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30   99) \subsection{The Political Economy of Zoning Friction and Participatory Distortion}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  100) 
f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  101) The foundational context for this research rests on the consensus that land-use regulation functions as a binding constraint on housing supply, driving a wedge between fundamental construction costs and market prices \cite{glaeser2005manhattan, glaeser2003zoning}. While macro-economic literature establishes the downstream effects of this regulatory tax---including suppressed economic growth and diminished housing affordability---urban planning scholarship points to the local political economy to explain why such constraints persist despite citywide needs \cite{gyourko2015regulation}. 
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  102) 
f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  103) The theoretical centerpiece for understanding this localized resistance is Fischel's \cite{fischel2001homevoter} homevoter hypothesis. Fischel posits that homeowners engage in local politics primarily to protect both the use value and exchange value of their homes, rationally utilizing zoning restrictions to preserve neighborhood exclusivity and mitigate perceived financial risks. However, recent empirical work has demonstrated that this protective impulse translates into a highly asymmetric political arena. Einstein, Palmer, and Glick \cite{einstein2019book} document the phenomenon of ``participatory distortion,'' demonstrating that the participants who dominate planning and zoning board meetings are overwhelmingly older, disproportionately white, longtime homeowners who are united in opposing new housing. These ``neighborhood defenders'' possess the time, resources, and localized knowledge to effectively block individual projects. Furthermore, Hankinson \cite{hankinson2018} complicates the pure asset-protection motive by revealing that renters in high-cost cities exhibit spatial NIMBYism on par with homeowners due to localized displacement anxiety, cementing the reality that spatial proximity to development triggers mobilization regardless of tenure.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  104) 
84859ac0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  105) \subsection{Discretionary Review and Institutional Vetoes}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  106) 
f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  107) Participatory distortion matters for housing supply when it operates through institutions that can delay or block projects. Manville and Monkkonen \cite{manville2016unpermitted} highlight that discretionary review processes---where development relies on site-specific municipal approvals rather than by-right administrative clearance---serve as the primary vehicle through which local opposition exerts leverage. In a discretionary regime, citywide housing goals are repeatedly displaced by parcel-level political conflict \cite{hills2011zoning}.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  108) 
290c7b8f (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  109) In Texas, this leverage is explicitly codified through the protest petition mechanism (Local Government Code \S~211.006). The statute legally empowers adjacent property owners to force supermajority city council voting requirements if owners representing 20\% of the \emph{area of lots} immediately adjoining the proposed change and extending 200 feet from that area sign a petition that the City Clerk certifies as valid.\footnote{\S~211.006(d) and (f) were repealed effective September 1, 2025 by HB 24 (89th Texas Legislature). The operative provisions now reside in new \S~211.0061, which establishes a three-tier protest system: (1)~owners of $\ge$20\% of the directly affected area trigger a three-fourths vote; (2)~owners of $\ge$60\% of the adjoining 200-foot buffer trigger a three-fourths vote; and (3)~for qualifying residential development under subsection~(b)(3), a simple majority of all governing body members is required. Proposed comprehensive zoning changes are exempted from the protest mechanism entirely.} This mechanism empowers localized opposition, transforming protest into a formal institutional hurdle. Cellini, Ferreira, and Rothstein \cite{cellini2010schools}, in their analysis of California school bond referenda, demonstrate how supermajority thresholds inherently distort political outcomes to favor the status quo. 
0786d29e (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  110) 
b35c2741 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  111) In Austin, the protest petition creates institutional friction for land use: cases exceeding the measured petition threshold appear to face additional voting requirements and longer processing times. Recent empirical research by the Mercatus Center at George Mason University confirms the severity and asymmetry of this mechanism specifically within the Austin political geography. Their analysis demonstrates that measured threshold-crossing petitions are more common for rezonings that increase residential density: historically, 25\% of rezonings to multifamily use in Austin crossed that measured threshold, compared to only 5\% for commercial rezonings.

7f8713c7 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  136) \subsection{Prediction Framing in Spatial Planning}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  137) 
f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  138) Traditional econometric scholarship in urban planning almost exclusively evaluates the causal effect of zoning---for instance, whether upzoning increases land values or whether a protest petition causes delay. However, addressing the localized gridlock of discretionary review requires anticipating \emph{where} opposition will emerge before political capital is expended. This intellectual justification builds on Shmueli's \cite{shmueli2010} foundational distinction between explanatory modeling (testing causal hypotheses) and predictive modeling (generating accurate forecasts of new observations), and on Mullainathan and Spiess \cite{mullainathan2017ml}, who argue that machine learning solves a fundamentally different problem than traditional econometrics: prediction ($\hat{y}$) rather than parameter estimation ($\hat{\beta}$).
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  139) 
8deaddff (copilot-swe-agent[bot] 2026-04-14  140) Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan, and Obermeyer \cite{kleinberg2015prediction} formalize this distinction by defining the ``predictive policy question,'' cases where optimal policy decisions depend not on understanding the causal mechanism of an outcome, but on accurately forecasting its occurrence. Predicting which zoning cases will meet the petition threshold is one such question. Planners and policymakers need to know which projects will trigger supermajority voting requirements to efficiently allocate political capital, engage in proactive community mediation, or design comprehensive reforms that bypass parcel-level friction \cite{athey2019ml}. This research tests whether administrative, spatial, and neighborhood features observable at the time of filing carry sufficient signal to anticipate this specific institutional event.

a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  259) \subsection{Primary Outcome Definition}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  260) \label{sec:outcome}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  261) 
290c7b8f (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  262) The primary predictive outcome ($y_0$) is a \emph{measured threshold-crossing petition}: a binary indicator equal to one when the reconstructed petition-share calculation---derived from public petition filings and buffer-area computations---exceeds the 20\% statutory threshold defined by Texas Local Government Code Chapter~211 and Austin LDC \S~25-2-284. a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  373)   \subsection{Model Evaluation Strategy}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  374) 
7d8582f2 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-12  375) Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC) is the primary discrimination metric, given the substantial class imbalance of the outcome. Following standard methodological practices in rare-event spatial forecasting, traditional Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) metrics are omitted entirely, as the large number of true negatives artificially inflates the curve. PR-AUC explicitly isolates the minority class, forcing the model to balance recall against the precision cost of false alarms.
7d8582f2 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-12  376) 
7f8713c7 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  377) \textbf{Primary evaluation.} The out-of-distribution bootstrap PR-AUC at the filing-date horizon is the primary evaluation object, as it approximates forward-like administrative use. In-distribution 5-fold cross-validation is retained only as an optimistic upper-bound object. Because this is primarily a ranking exercise, emphasis remains on ranking discrimination (PR-AUC, top-decile lift), with calibration and thresholded diagnostics treated as supporting information rather than coequal headline estimands. Seed-based audits are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:seed_stability} as variance diagnostics.
7f8713c7 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  378) 
7f8713c7 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  379) \textbf{Metric-object reconciliation.} The manuscript reports multiple metric objects for different inferential purposes, and they are not treated as interchangeable: (i) the headline Stage~C object is OOD bootstrap at filing date, (ii) the 20-seed mean characterizes within-sample stochastic variance, (iii) in-distribution cross-validation is an optimistic upper bound, and (iv) calibration-method comparisons in Appendix~\ref{app:alt_models} are method-screening objects, not headline estimates. A manifest reconciliation layer maps manuscript macros to the corresponding object metadata (task, split, model, calibration state, and source artifact) in \texttt{reporting/final\_metrics\_manifest.json}.
3d722a59 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  380) 
7f8713c7 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  381) Because the baseline opposition frequency is highly skewed, raw predicted probabilities are calibration-sensitive. The evaluation therefore applies out-of-fold Isotonic Regression and reports calibration summaries (ECE, Brier, ACE = \metricACE{}).\footnote{At a classification threshold of $P \geq 0.50$, the model achieves precision of \metricPrecisionAtFifty{} and recall of \metricRecallAtFifty{}. This threshold is included for diagnostics only; ranking-oriented review budgets are the primary decision frame for rare-event triage.} The Brier score is \metricBrierScore{}.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  382) 
f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  383) Strictly proper scoring rules like the Brier Score \cite{brier1950verification} and Adaptive Calibration (which evaluates equal-mass rather than equal-width bins) are useful complementary calibration measures; standard ECE can be unstable under severe class imbalance because fixed-width probability bins at the highest confidence intervals routinely remain empty when predicting rare events, causing aggregate ECE weights to understate miscalibration for rare positive cases.

a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  771) \section{Conclusion}
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  772) 
b99510ae (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  773) Filing-date administrative features can rank petition risk in Austin's pre-HB\,24 zoning regime, but they do not yet yield calibrated probabilities suitable for institutional use.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  774) 
776a2a71 (copilot-swe-agent[bot] 2026-04-14  775) This thesis asks whether measured threshold-crossing petitions against housing development proposals can be predicted before formal public hearings. The answer is qualified: spatial, economic, and site characteristics carry predictive information about petition risk, but the model does not meet the calibration benchmarks required for institutional use. Under the primary evaluation---out-of-distribution bootstrap at the filing-date horizon---the model achieves a PR-AUC of \metricBootstrapFiling{} (95\% CI: \metricBootstrapFilingCI{}), with primary-layer calibration error (Layer C1 ECE = \metricECEOODBootstrapCalibrated{}) exceeding the 0.05 threshold. In-distribution cross-validation provides an upper bound PR-AUC of \metricPRAUC{}. A regression discontinuity at the 20\% petition threshold estimates a \metricRDDelay{}-day increase in council processing time, consistent with the institutional significance of the outcome measure. Descriptive evidence from the 2022 electoral transition suggests that petition filing rates declined in districts that changed political representation, though staggered treatment timing and few treated clusters preclude formal causal claims.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  776) 
dabdd2a7 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  777) These results point to a ceiling on what prediction from public records alone can achieve. Petition filing is partly driven by factors not observable at the time of filing---neighborhood social networks, informal political dynamics, media attention---and the 2022 council regime change altered the relationship between petitions and outcomes. Interview interpretation further contextualizes an ethical limitation: the model cannot distinguish affluent exclusionary mobilization from community-driven anti-displacement resistance, a distinction that administrative data does not encode.
a166962a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  778) 
53bf5b30 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-13  779) Future work should extend the observation window to capture HB~24 effects \cite{hb24_2025}, explore richer text features from pre-hearing public comments, and isolate the exact mechanisms of the 2021--2022 temporary covariate shock.

4baaa4d5 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  981) To calculate the False Negative Rate (FNR) gaps:
4baaa4d5 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  982) \begin{itemize}
4baaa4d5 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  983)     \item \textbf{Geography:} Subgroups defined by the 10 Austin City Council Districts.
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  984)     \item \textbf{Socioeconomic Vulnerability:} Subgroups defined by the UT-Austin Uprooted Displacement Risk index (Vulnerable, Active, Chronic).\footnote{Categories follow the 2024 City of Austin update. f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  988) \subsection{Natural Language Processing Model: Exploratory Text Features}
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  989) 
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  990) Text signals from public hearings post-date the initial filing date prediction horizon and are therefore explored only at later administrative stages. Because these features are unavailable at the time of filing, they are treated as exploratory supplements rather than inputs to the primary opposition model.
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  991) 
1a4d7999 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  992) The methodology proceeds in three stages:
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  993) 
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  994) \begin{enumerate}
e7f5ad18 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  995)     \item \textbf{Transcription.} Audio recordings of City Council and Planning Commission hearings are transcribed locally using Whisper (faster-whisper). Each transcript is linked to its originating zoning case number, producing a corpus of \metricNLPCorpus{} case-level text documents.
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  996) 
e7f5ad18 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05  997)     \item \textbf{Seed labeling via large language model.} A randomized sample of \metricNLPSeed{} transcripts is classified by a locally hosted language model (Llama 3.2, 3B parameters). For each transcript, the model is prompted to return two outputs:
14d51f8a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30  998)     \begin{itemize}
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31  999)         \item \textbf{Stance:} Whether the overall testimony is supportive, opposed, or neutral toward the proposed zoning change.
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31 1000)         \item \textbf{Argument frames:} Which of five predefined thematic categories appear in the testimony: (1)~traffic and congestion, (2)~infrastructure capacity, (3)~displacement and affordability, (4)~neighborhood character and compatibility, and (5)~procedural fairness. These categories were selected based on recurring themes identified in the urban planning literature on participatory opposition.
14d51f8a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30 1001)     \end{itemize}
e7f5ad18 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05 1002)     The language model responses are parsed into structured binary labels (frame present or absent) and a three-class stance label. These \metricNLPSeed{} labeled cases serve as the training seed.
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31 1003) 
e7f5ad18 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05 1004)     \item \textbf{Supervised expansion across the full corpus.} Using the \metricNLPSeed{} seed labels as training data, six logistic regression classifiers are trained---one for stance and one for each of the five argument frames---with TF-IDF unigram features (1,000-term vocabulary, English stop words removed) as inputs. Each classifier then predicts a continuous probability for every case in the full \metricNLPCorpus{} transcript corpus. The result is a case-level matrix of six predicted probabilities: the likelihood that the hearing testimony is oppositional, and the likelihood that each argument frame is present.
5de3b6b0 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-31 1005) \end{enumerate}
14d51f8a (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-03-30 1006) 
f5d47627 (Daniel Hardesty Lewis  2026-04-05 1007) This approach is intentionally lightweight. The seed sample is small, at \metricNLPSeed{} cases, and the classifiers use simple linear models over bag-of-words features. Consequently, the predicted probabilities should be interpreted as coarse thematic indicators rather than precise measurements. The goal is to test whether even rough text signals carry discriminative power for opposition prediction at later horizons, not to build a comprehensive NLP model.
