\begin{abstract}
This thesis asks whether formal protest petitions against housing development proposals in Austin, Texas can be ranked by risk before formal public hearings, using only information available at the time of filing. The outcome is a \emph{measured threshold-crossing petition}---an operationalized indicator of whether the reconstructed petition-share calculation exceeded the statutory 20\% threshold in public records. Because municipal clerk certification is not directly observed, this measure is distinct from a legally certified measured threshold-crossing petition. Working from \metricBaselineCases{} discretionary zoning cases filed under Austin's pre-HB\,24 regime (2007--2024), out-of-distribution bootstrap evaluation at the filing-date horizon yields a PR-AUC of \metricBootstrapFiling{} (95\% CI: \metricBootstrapFilingCI{}), with primary-layer calibration error $\text{ECE}_{C1}=\metricECEOODBootstrapCalibrated{}$ after Isotonic Regression---above the 0.05 threshold for reliable probabilistic interpretation. Filing-date administrative features can rank petition risk, but they do not yet yield portable, well-calibrated probabilities for administrative deployment. By applying discriminatory ranking to administrative municipal data... testing whether administrative spatial characteristics carry sufficient discriminatory signal to anticipate formal protest petitions...
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

As the United States confronts a persistent housing affordability crisis, understanding local barriers to new housing supply remains an important empirical challenge. Traditional accounts emphasize the role of municipal zoning laws, but a substantial share of delay and blockage occurs through discretionary political processes---public hearings, protest petitions, and council negotiations---that collectively slow or block individual projects. This thesis uses Austin, Texas as a case study. Between 2010 and 2024, rapid employment growth drove severe housing supply constraints, inflating property values and generating sustained conflict between neighborhood preservation and regional development goals. The study is historically bounded to Austin's pre-HB\,24 protest petition regime (2007--2024) and serves as a template for analyzing institutional change in local land-use governance, not as a near-term operational tool under current Texas law.

Against this backdrop, the central research question is whether a measured threshold-crossing petition---defined below in Section~\ref{sec:outcome}---can be predicted before formal public hearings begin.

The outcome under study is specific. Under the pre-2025 framework of Texas Local Government Code \S~211.006 \cite{texas211006}, adjacent property owners may file a protest petition; if signatures representing 20\% of the relevant adjoining land area were collected and the municipal clerk certified the petition as valid, the petition historically required a three-fourths supermajority for City Council approval. (Effective September 1, 2025, House Bill 24 repealed and replaced this mechanism with a differentiated, multi-tier system under \S~211.0061.) This is one institutionalized form of neighborhood opposition---the most consequential and best-observed form in the Austin regulatory context---not a prediction of opposition in all its forms. The thesis narrows its claim accordingly: the primary contribution is an assessment of whether administrative, spatial, and economic features observable at the time of a zoning application are sufficient to anticipate this specific institutional event.

To answer this question, the research constructs a sequential predictive methodology organized around four stages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage A---Development Occurrence:} Where housing development proposals are likely to emerge, modeled as a discrete-time site hazard across \metricBaselineParcels{} candidate parcels.
    \item \textbf{Stage B---Project Type and Scale:} Conditional on a development event, the expected project form and unit count.
    \item \textbf{Stage C---Formal Protest Petition:} Conditional on a project entering the formal public process, the probability that a measured threshold-crossing petition is filed. \textit{This is the core of the thesis.}
    \item \textbf{Stage D---Political Outcome Analysis:} Conditional on opposition status, establishing structural data limits and the high rate of pre-council withdrawal among protested cases.
\end{itemize}

Stages A, B, and D provide context for Stage~C. Stage~A supplies inverse-probability weights to adjust for spatial selection into the analytic sample. Stage~B characterizes the heterogeneous institutional responses associated with different project types. Stage~D documents the downstream consequences of petition filing, including high rates of pre-council withdrawal among opposed cases. The predictive evaluation, however, centers on Stage~C: whether the model can discriminate petition risk at the filing-date horizon and whether it does so with sufficient calibration and equity to support high-confidence administrative use.

The thesis also includes two exploratory analyses of downstream institutional dynamics. A regression discontinuity design at the 20\% petition threshold estimates the processing-time consequence of crossing the statutory margin. Descriptive evidence from the 2022 council electoral transition, in which specific districts replaced preservationist incumbents with pro-housing representatives, examines whether petition filing rates declined differentially in those districts.

Finally, semi-structured interviews with planning professionals, conducted under IRB Protocol ACYY0820, informed the interpretation of quantitative findings by surfacing institutional dynamics---informal negotiations, strategic petition timing, neighborhood network mobilization---that administrative data cannot directly observe. The interviews do not validate the model; they contextualize findings within professional planning practice and surface governance concerns about potential misuse of predictive outputs.

\section{Literature Review}

This literature review synthesizes scholarship across three core themes to directly motivate the thesis's methodological approach. It first establishes the political economy of homeowner opposition and participatory distortion. Second, it isolates the specific institutional mechanism of the protest petition. Finally, it provides the intellectual justification for treating zoning conflict as primarily a predictive policy problem while acknowledging the inherent evaluative constraints required for administrative use of predictive models in spatial planning.

\subsection{The Political Economy of Zoning Friction and Participatory Distortion}

The foundational context for this research rests on the consensus that land-use regulation functions as a binding constraint on housing supply, driving a wedge between fundamental construction costs and market prices \cite{glaeser2005manhattan, glaeser2003zoning}. While macro-economic literature establishes the downstream effects of this regulatory tax---including suppressed economic growth and diminished housing affordability---urban planning scholarship points to the local political economy to explain why such constraints persist despite citywide needs \cite{gyourko2015regulation}. 

The theoretical centerpiece for understanding this localized resistance is Fischel's \cite{fischel2001homevoter} homevoter hypothesis. Fischel posits that homeowners engage in local politics primarily to protect both the use value and exchange value of their homes, rationally utilizing zoning restrictions to preserve neighborhood exclusivity and mitigate perceived financial risks. However, recent empirical work has demonstrated that this protective impulse translates into a highly asymmetric political arena. Einstein, Palmer, and Glick \cite{einstein2019book} document the phenomenon of ``participatory distortion,'' demonstrating that the participants who dominate planning and zoning board meetings are overwhelmingly older, disproportionately white, longtime homeowners who are united in opposing new housing. These ``neighborhood defenders'' possess the time, resources, and localized knowledge to effectively block individual projects. Furthermore, Hankinson \cite{hankinson2018} complicates the pure asset-protection motive by revealing that renters in high-cost cities exhibit spatial NIMBYism on par with homeowners due to localized displacement anxiety, cementing the reality that spatial proximity to development triggers mobilization regardless of tenure.

\subsection{Discretionary Review and Institutional Vetoes}

Participatory distortion matters for housing supply when it operates through institutions that can delay or block projects. Manville and Monkkonen \cite{manville2016unpermitted} highlight that discretionary review processes---where development relies on site-specific municipal approvals rather than by-right administrative clearance---serve as the primary vehicle through which local opposition exerts leverage. In a discretionary regime, citywide housing goals are repeatedly displaced by parcel-level political conflict \cite{hills2011zoning}.

In Texas, this leverage is explicitly codified through the protest petition mechanism (Local Government Code \S~211.006). The statute legally empowers adjacent property owners to force supermajority city council voting requirements if owners representing 20\% of the \emph{area of lots} immediately adjoining the proposed change and extending 200 feet from that area sign a valid petition.\footnote{\S~211.006(d) and (f) were repealed effective September 1, 2025 by HB 24 (89th Texas Legislature). The operative provisions now reside in new \S~211.0061, which establishes a three-tier protest system: (1)~owners of $\ge$20\% of the directly affected area trigger a three-fourths vote; (2)~owners of $\ge$60\% of the adjoining 200-foot buffer trigger a three-fourths vote; and (3)~for qualifying residential development under subsection~(b)(3), a simple majority of all governing body members is required. Proposed comprehensive zoning changes are exempted from the protest mechanism entirely.} This mechanism empowers localized opposition, transforming protest into a formal institutional hurdle. Cellini, Ferreira, and Rothstein \cite{cellini2010schools}, in their analysis of California school bond referenda, demonstrate how supermajority thresholds inherently distort political outcomes to favor the status quo. 

In Austin, the protest petition creates institutional friction for land use: cases exceeding the measured petition threshold appear to face additional voting requirements and longer processing times. Recent empirical research by the Mercatus Center at George Mason University confirms the severity and asymmetry of this mechanism specifically within the Austin political geography. Their analysis demonstrates that measured threshold-crossing petitions are more common for rezonings that increase residential density: historically, 25\% of rezonings to multifamily use in Austin crossed that measured threshold, compared to only 5\% for commercial rezonings.

Because the process is fundamentally asymmetric---empowering opponents of development through a fixed statutory buffer geometry---it creates a bias toward the status quo, dragging contested housing applications into months of procedural delays. The Texas Legislature structurally altered this framework in 2025; under House Bill 24, the state established new \S~211.0061, which exempts proposed comprehensive zoning changes from the protest mechanism entirely and replaces the uniform 20\% trigger with a differentiated, multi-tier system. For qualifying residential protests under subsection~(b)(3), the vote consequence is a simple majority of all governing body members rather than the prior three-fourths supermajority; for other protest paths, three-fourths remains the threshold. Consequently, predicting whether a measured threshold-crossing petition will be filed against an application under the pre-HB\,24 regime forms the core empirical challenge of this thesis.

\subsection{The Prediction Policy Problem in Spatial Planning}

Traditional econometric scholarship in urban planning almost exclusively evaluates the causal effect of zoning---for instance, whether upzoning increases land values or whether a protest petition causes delay. However, addressing the localized gridlock of discretionary review requires anticipating \emph{where} opposition will emerge before political capital is expended. This intellectual justification builds on Shmueli's \cite{shmueli2010} foundational distinction between explanatory modeling (testing causal hypotheses) and predictive modeling (generating accurate forecasts of new observations), and on Mullainathan and Spiess \cite{mullainathan2017ml}, who argue that machine learning solves a fundamentally different problem than traditional econometrics: prediction ($\hat{y}$) rather than parameter estimation ($\hat{\beta}$).

Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan, and Obermeyer \cite{kleinberg2015prediction} formalize this distinction by defining the ``predictive policy question,'' cases where optimal policy decisions depend not on understanding the causal mechanism of an outcome, but on accurately forecasting its occurrence. Predicting which zoning cases will meet the petition threshold can be framed as a predictive policy question. Planners and policymakers need to know which projects will trigger supermajority voting requirements to efficiently allocate political capital, engage in proactive community mediation, or design comprehensive reforms that bypass parcel-level friction \cite{athey2019ml}. By applying algorithmic forecasting to administrative municipal data, this research transitions the study of NIMBYism from a retrospective causal analysis into a proactive predictive model, testing whether administrative spatial characteristics carry sufficient signal to anticipate formal protest petitions in formal proceedings weeks or months before a public hearing takes place.

\subsection{Evaluative Constraints on Predictive Governance}

While transitioning to a predictive methodology offers potential analytical value for municipal planning, it introduces serious ethical risks. Barocas and Selbst \cite{barocas2016} demonstrate that predictive algorithms inevitably inherit the prejudices embedded in their training data. Because historical zoning data in American cities is inextricably linked to legacies of redlining, racial covenants, and exclusionary zoning \cite{whittemore2014racial}, the underlying spatial features, such as lot geometry, single-family zoning, and historic property values, function as proxies for race and class. If an algorithm learns that wealthy, historically white neighborhoods successfully file protest petitions, using that prediction to guide development decisions risks encoding a feedback loop that shields those neighborhoods from densification while concentrating development pressure on politically vulnerable communities \cite{richardson2019dirty}.

Consequently, the viability of algorithmic prediction in spatial planning is constrained by mathematical limits on fairness. Chouldechova \cite{chouldechova2017fair} proves that when base rates of an outcome differ across groups, no imperfect predictor can simultaneously satisfy both predictive parity and classification parity. Recognizing these fixed mathematical constraints, modern predictive models intended for public-sector use must be evaluated against explicit calibration and error-rate thresholds. Rather than attempting to maximize pure predictive lift, the administrative application of such models should consider explicit calibration accuracy and the equalization of False Negative Rates across geographic groups, ensuring that predictive efficiency does not come at the cost of democratic equity.

\section{Data and Methods}

The empirical foundation is a time-stamped spatial panel dataset that reconstructs what information was available at each historical prediction point. Every record anchors to an explicit as-of date, ensuring that backtests use only Census releases, appraisal rolls, and case documents published before the prediction date.

\subsection{Analytical Panel Structure}

The core analytics rely on the filing-date analytic dataset, which merges spatial and demographic variables into single case-level observations (with temporally matched covariates):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zoning Application Records:} Case ID, milestone dates (filing, notice, petition deadline, planning commission hearing, council reading, withdrawal), requested zoning changes, unit limits, target FAR, and disposition status.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Geometries:} Parcel IDs, geometries, acreage, frontage, and 200ft buffers used to aggregate nearby properties (council districts and overlapping geometries).
    \item \textbf{Parcel Appraisals:} Annually indexed TCAD appraisal variables for parcels used to compute variables within the 200-foot buffer: land value, structure age, homestead exemption rates, and owner-occupancy shares.
    \item \textbf{Neighborhood Demographics:} Tract and block group ACS variables constrained to their historical 5-year release dates: rent burdens, tenure splits, demographic composition, and displacement vulnerability indicators.
    \item \textbf{Institutional Calendar:} The 2022 council regime change; the HB 24 effective date (September 1, 2025).
\end{enumerate}

Because municipal databases frequently overwrite preliminary timestamps with final disposition dates, precise milestone dates are reconstructed through two methods. First, explicit dates are parsed from scraped historical council agendas and PDF case files. Second, where explicit text is unavailable, dates are imputed by back-calculating from end-dates using the minimum procedural constraints mandated by Texas Local Government Code and Austin municipal zoning ordinances, such as statutory 11-day mail notice minimums.

Supporting tables log meeting events (staff and commission recommendations), speech comments (speaker stance and segmented text), petition records (validity and signature counts), and vote records (case-by-councilmember dynamics).

The analytical samples vary by analytical goal. The primary predictive evaluation (Stage C) uses the full sample of \metricBaselineCases{} discretionary geographic cases. In contrast, the supporting analyses use restricted sub-samples designed to isolate specific mechanisms. The 2022 electoral-transition descriptive analysis and the HOME event study utilize $N=518$ because they are restricted to active single-member districts immediately surrounding the relevant election or policy cycle. The Invariant Causal Prediction (ICP) analysis utilizes $N=1,186$ multi-parcel geometric environments to represent cohesive spatial clusters. Finally, the institutional geographic overlay regressions evaluate policy flags aggregated at the council-term level across periods, resulting in $N=20$ macro-observations.

Unlike approaches that require complete-case geometric intersections, this methodology uses CatBoost's native missing-value handling to build splits over absent variables, such as unmatched TCAD boundaries, without listwise deletion. This approach retains cases with partially missing covariates, reducing artificial attrition and the selection bias introduced by demanding full covariate coverage. Whether it also improves fairness metrics is a separate empirical question explored in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Primary Outcome Definition}
\label{sec:outcome}

The primary predictive outcome ($y_0$) is a \emph{measured threshold-crossing petition}: a binary indicator equal to one when the reconstructed petition-share calculation---derived from public petition filings and buffer-area computations---exceeds the 20\% statutory threshold defined by Texas Local Government Code Chapter~211 and Austin LDC \S~25-2-284. This is an operationalized outcome, not a direct observation of the legally decisive event. The City Clerk's formal certification of each petition as legally valid is not recorded in the public dataset; consequently, the outcome captures cases that \emph{appear} to have crossed the threshold based on available records, which may include petitions that were ultimately rejected on procedural grounds or petitions whose true area share differed from the calculated value. Throughout this manuscript, ``threshold-crossing petition'' refers to this reconstructed measure, and ``legally certified petition'' is reserved for the unobserved clerk-certified outcome.

Secondary outcomes are analyzed separately:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $y_1$: Public hearing opposition intensity (number of opposing speakers)
    \item $y_2$: Negative staff recommendation
    \item $y_3$: Planning commission dissent (split vote or denial)
    \item $y_4$: Council outcome (delay, denial, or unit dilution)
    \item $y_5$: Petition signature count (continuous)
\end{itemize}

All primary results report $y_0$. Sensitivity analyses replicate key findings using individual secondary outcomes. This separation is important because staff recommendations, commission votes, and council outcomes reflect different institutional mechanisms than neighborhood mobilization through petitions, and collapsing them into a single binary would obscure meaningful distinctions. Cases withdrawn before public notice are excluded, rather than coded as zero, because no opportunity for opposition existed. ETJ cases and routine variances are excluded because the formal petition mechanism does not apply to them.

Note on stage-specific samples: the Stage C filing-date model uses the full \metricBaselineCases{}-case analytic sample regardless of whether a hearing transcript exists, since transcripts post-date the filing horizon and are not used as predictive features at that stage. Cases missing hearing transcripts or petition-database coverage are omitted only from later-horizon evaluations and secondary-outcome analyses where those records serve as inputs or outcome sources.

\subsection{Feature Libraries: Policy Forecasting vs.\ Explanatory Research}

The feature set is separated to prevent predictive models from leveraging sensitive attributes:

\textbf{Predictive features} include physical site characteristics (lot area, unit count changes, PUD/TOD flags), statutory eligibility flags (HB 24 status, owner-vs.-city-initiated), aggregated buffer economics (homestead shares, land-to-improvement ratios), broad demographic indicators (median income, renter share), historical petition rates within the council district, and macroeconomic variables (mortgage rates, vacancy rates). These elements are selected strictly based on their availability in public administrative and appraisal databases prior to the initial filing date, ensuring the algorithm's decisions replicate constraints faced by municipal planning staff in real time.

\textbf{Explanatory research features} are restricted to equity analysis. These include tract-level racial and ethnic composition, segregation indices, and probabilistic surname-based racial classification. These features are strictly partitioned from the predictive model to reduce the risk of encoding disparate impacts, though proxy correlation through permitted features remains a concern (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). By reserving these variables exclusively for post-hoc fairness evaluations, the model avoids generating predictions based on protected class attributes while maintaining the capability to measure intersectional disparities across geographic subgroups.



\section{Modeling Strategy}

\subsection{Supporting Stages: Development Occurrence, Project Type, and Political Outcome}
\label{sec:supporting_stages}

The predictive evaluation centers on Stage~C (Formal Protest Petition). Three supporting stages provide context and methodological inputs:

\textbf{Stage A---Development Occurrence} models where housing development proposals are likely to emerge as a discrete-time site hazard across \metricBaselineParcels{} candidate parcels. Its primary methodological role is to supply inverse-probability weights (IPW) that adjust for spatial selection into the analytic sample: the filing-date petition model observes only neighborhoods that actually received development proposals, and IPW corrects for this non-random selection on observables. Full model details, target definitions, precision--recall curves, and hotspot maps are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:stagea}.

\textbf{Stage B---Project Type and Scale} classifies the expected project form and estimates expected unit counts, conditional on a development event. This segmentation is important because different project types (e.g., six-unit by-right infill vs.\ 300-unit PUD) generate fundamentally different institutional responses and petition likelihoods. Classification performance is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:stageb}.

\textbf{Stage D---Political Outcome Analysis} documents the structural missingness and high rates of pre-council withdrawal conditional on a threshold-crossing petition. Because virtually all cases that survive to a final council vote are approved, pre-council attrition is the primary mechanism through which opposition friction manifests. However, due to severe data censorship of withdrawn cases, it cannot be modeled predictively. This analysis is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:staged}.

A composite measure combining all four stages (\textbf{expected contested units}) is also constructed and mapped in Appendix~\ref{app:contested_units}. Because this multiplicative structure compounds uncertainty from each stage, the resulting map should be interpreted as a relative ranking rather than a reliable absolute probability.

\subsection{Formal Protest Petition}

The Formal Protest Petition forecast is the core of the thesis: conditional on a project entering the formal public process, predicting the probability of a measured threshold-crossing petition ($y_0 = 1$):
\begin{equation}
P(y_0 = 1 \mid D=1, X_{i,t}, Z)
\end{equation}

At the initial filing date horizon, the predictive feature space ($X_{i,t}$) is restricted to information observable by planners at the time of filing, including site geometry, requested zoning changes, buffer demographics, and historical petition rates. To adjust for selection on observables---given that the analytic sample naturally contains only neighborhoods that actually receive development proposals---the model applies inverse-probability weighting derived from the Development Occurrence hazard ($Z$). While this weighting corrects for spatial baseline probabilities, it remains inherently limited because it cannot completely resolve unobserved political or social factors that simultaneously influence both proposal location and opposition likelihood. Precision-recall curves comparing the CatBoost, Random Forest, ElasticNet Logistic, and V-REx models are reported in Figure~\ref{fig:pr_curves}.

  \subsection{Model Evaluation Strategy}

Precision-Recall AUC (PR-AUC) is the primary discrimination metric, given the substantial class imbalance of the outcome. Following standard methodological practices in rare-event spatial forecasting, traditional Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) metrics are omitted entirely, as the large number of true negatives artificially inflates the curve. PR-AUC explicitly isolates the minority class, forcing the model to balance recall against the precision cost of false alarms.

\textbf{Primary evaluation.} The out-of-distribution bootstrap PR-AUC at the filing-date horizon is the primary evaluation metric, as it simulates realistic forward evaluation conditions. In-distribution 5-fold cross-validation PR-AUC is reported as an upper bound. Because this is a ranking model with limited calibration transportability, the emphasis is on ranking metrics (PR-AUC, top-decile lift), calibration plots, and decision-relevant review budgets rather than threshold-dependent classification metrics. To verify that reported metrics are not artifacts of pseudo-random seed selection, all stochastic models are validated across 20 independent seeds; the resulting confidence intervals ($\sigma_{\text{PR-AUC}} < 0.05$ for all architectures) are reported in Table~\ref{tab:comprehensive_benchmark} and Appendix~\ref{app:seed_stability}.

Because the baseline opposition frequency is highly skewed, raw predicted probabilities from gradient boosting models suffer from persistent overconfidence. To address this, the evaluation applies out-of-fold Isotonic Regression, which fits a non-decreasing, non-parametric monotonic function mapping predicted probabilities to empirical outcome frequencies, enforcing calibration within the evaluation loop. Alongside standard Expected Calibration Error (ECE), the evaluation also reports the Brier Score and Adaptive Calibration Error (ACE = \metricACE{}).\footnote{At a classification threshold of $P \geq 0.50$, the model achieves precision of \metricPrecisionAtFifty{} and recall of \metricRecallAtFifty{}, flagging \metricNFlaggedAtFifty{} of \metricBaselineCases{} cases. However, this threshold is difficult to defend for a rare-event triage problem; ranking-based metrics are more appropriate.} The Brier score is \metricBrierScore{} (\metricCBFilingBrier{} across 20 seeds). 

Strictly proper scoring rules like the Brier Score \cite{brier1950verification} and Adaptive Calibration (which evaluates equal-mass rather than equal-width bins) are useful complementary calibration measures; standard ECE can be unstable under severe class imbalance because fixed-width probability bins at the highest confidence intervals routinely remain empty when predicting rare events, causing aggregate ECE weights to understate miscalibration for rare positive cases.

The mean predicted probability for true positive cases is \metricMeanProbTP{}, compared to \metricMeanProbTN{} for true negatives. Evaluation splits are intentionally non-random to test generalization under realistic administrative application conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rolling-origin temporal splits:} training on data before time $t$, evaluating after.
    \item \textbf{Policy-regime holdouts:} partitioning the data to evaluate whether structural failures in predictive accuracy match discrete eras of council shifts and COVID-19 demographic shocks.
    \item \textbf{Spatial holdouts:} withholding entire council districts.
\end{itemize}

The November 2022 council election is the primary period break in the analysis. The election produced a more pro-housing council majority during the 2023--2024 term, possessing the supermajority (typically 9 votes, though fewer may be required if a council member is recused or absent) to override protest petitions \cite{tcclerk2022}. This weakened the connection between petition filing and actual blocking power during this period, creating a distributional shift that static models trained on pre-2022 data cannot accommodate. Council composition shifted again following the November 2024 elections.

\textbf{Temporal Predictive Drift (Rolling Origin).} To quantify the model's predictive half-life, Table~\ref{tab:temporal_drift} tracks PR-AUC across expanding annual windows for three architectures: CatBoost, TabNet with structural sparse pruning (\texttt{LS+Pruning}), and TabNet with label smoothing only (\texttt{LS=0.1}). For each anchor year $t$, each model is trained on data before $t$ and evaluated forward across all subsequent evaluation years. Bold denotes the top-performing model for each (anchor, forecast year) pairing; horizontal rules separate the three model blocks to facilitate direct within-anchor comparison.

CatBoost claims \textbf{19 of 28} comparable cells (68\%), and its margin of dominance is structurally consistent: across every anchor, CatBoost maintains discrimination in the 2022 and 2023 evaluation windows that both TabNet variants fail to sustain. TabNet's 9-cell minority is analytically interpretable rather than a sign of competitive parity. Two specific regimes account for virtually all of TabNet's wins. First, the \textit{pandemic trough}: TabNet(LS=0.1) outperforms CatBoost in the 2021--2022 evaluation windows from the Pre-2019 anchor (0.603 vs.\ 0.496 in 2021; 0.570 vs.\ 0.329 in 2022), suggesting that label smoothing confers short-term OOD robustness during acute covariate shift---a period when CatBoost's learned decision boundaries were most disrupted. Second, the \textit{recency-anchored 2024 recovery}: TabNet(LS+Pruning) leads in 2024 from the Pre-2021 and Pre-2022 anchors (0.836 and 0.839), suggesting that when the training window is restricted to the post-transition regime, TabNet's attention mechanism adapts its feature weighting faster than CatBoost. Critically, from older, longer-horizon anchors---where the V-shaped recovery is the primary test---CatBoost dominates the 2024 window by a wide margin (0.885 vs.\ 0.197 from the Pre-2019 anchor).

This V-shaped recovery is the central temporal finding: CatBoost's Pre-2019 anchor decays from 0.659 at immediate horizon (2019, T+0) to 0.329 at the 2022 window (T+3)---a sharp predictive cliff---before fully rebounding to 0.885 in 2024 (T+5). The recovery demonstrates that the 2021--2022 performance drop was a temporary out-of-distribution covariate shock rather than a permanent regime severance caused by the 2022 council flip. The underlying structural mechanics of opposition petitioning survived the transition intact; what failed temporarily was the model's calibration to a transient distributional shift, not a change in the fundamental petition-driving features. \textbf{The structural stability of these features during this transition is further verified by the longitudinal attribution audit in Appendix~\ref{app:unclustered_dynamic}.}

\textbf{Methodological Integrity and Pre-training Leakage.} The use of foundation models like TabPFN introduces a risk of "future leakage" if Austin's open data was inadvertently included in the model's original training corpus. This protocol ensures that the model's 0.54 PR-AUC success is a result of structural pattern recognition, not historical memorization.

\textbf{The Spurious Performance Trap.} While traditional tree-based architectures (Gradient Boosting, Random Forest) frequently top the raw out-of-distribution PR-AUC leaderboards in the Unified Predictive Frontier, this numerical dominance must be treated with severe methodological skepticism. \end{equation}

However, this meta-clustering explicitly rejects the notion of a ``democratic'' structural average, where all models are treated as equally valid representations of the true Data Generating Process (DGP). Traditional tree-based approaches (such as Gradient Boosting and Random Forests), while powerful for pure predictive triage, possess a greedy inductive bias: they optimize for immediate error reduction by latching onto spurious spatial proxies (e.g., heavily weighting raw parcel footprints simply because they map cleanly to wealthy single-family neighborhoods).

Conversely, architectures like V-REx are mathematically designed to isolate causal truth by explicitly penalizing reliance on variables that shift across computational environments. Therefore, the goal of this meta-analysis is not to find a naive compromise between architectures, but to isolate the true ``Invariant Core'': the exact set of features that survive \textit{despite} massive shifts in the underlying algorithmic lens. As the visualization demonstrates, features such as Neighborhood Demographics, Housing Tenure, and Neighborhood Income maintain consistently high predictive weight across \textit{both} standard predictive greed and strict causal penalization. \subsubsection{The Non-Stationary City and the Illusion of Static Portability}

The core finding of this empirical audit is that urban administrative environments are not stationary. Our taxonomic analysis demonstrated that the latent covariance structure of Austin's real estate data severely decoupled between 2018 and 2024 (evidenced by the Adjusted Rand Index dropping below 0.62). As the underlying reality of the city fractured under post-pandemic pressures, predictive models were forced to rapidly synthesize complex new interaction logic---ranging from CatBoost's socio-economic pairings to LightGBM's spatiotemporal coordinate splits---merely to stay afloat.

\textbf{The Policy Implication:} When municipal governments procure ``black-box'' urban risk models, they assume the model's logic is a fixed reflection of structural reality. This thesis proves the opposite: as the city's reality shifts, models quietly map entirely new, dynamic interaction pathways. Therefore, it is critical not just to monitor a model's predictive accuracy over time, but to explicitly audit its shifting attribution taxonomies. Without a frozen comparative baseline, planners will unwittingly accept automated decisions that are rapidly re-calibrating their biases to a changing urban grid. \textbf{Detailed longitudinal exhibits in Appendix~\ref{app:unclustered_dynamic} (Figures~\ref{fig:unclustered_dynamic} and~\ref{fig:clustered_dynamic}) demonstrate this phenomenon in practice, tracking how model focus diverges even as predictive performance fluctuates.}

To estimate the causal effect of crossing the formal protest threshold, a regression discontinuity (RD) design exploits the 20\% statutory petition threshold under Texas LGC Chapter~211. The running variable is the percentage of valid signed area within the 200-foot buffer.

A triangular-kernel weighted least squares estimator yields an estimated shift of $\metricRDDelay{}$ days in council processing time at the threshold ($p < 0.001$, SE $= \metricRDSE{}$, 95\% CI: $\metricRDCI{}$). This corresponds to approximately \metricRDDelayWeeks{} weeks of additional delay associated with crossing the statutory margin.

This estimate should be interpreted as a reduced-form effect at the threshold. Because the dataset does not record whether the City Clerk formally certified each petition as legally compliant, the analysis relies on an unverified calculated area share. Rather than assuming the exact threshold acts as a deterministic sharp trigger, the specification simply estimates the shift in average processing time associated with crossing the statutory 20\% margin. Consequently, this is a threshold-based reduced-form model where institutional compliance remains unobserved.

Design limitations include potential manipulation of the running variable, since petition organizers may strategically target just above 20\%, and instability in the post-2022 council environment where the supermajority was routinely available. Robustness checks---a McCrary (2008) density test for running-variable manipulation ($p > 0.05$), symmetric donut exclusions, and covariate continuity checks---support the validity of the design at the threshold.

\subsection{Descriptive Evidence from the 2022 Electoral Transition}

The 2022 council elections provide suggestive descriptive evidence on the relationship between political representation and petition filing. Two districts changed representation in ways that constitute a clear ideological shift: District~4 replaced its incumbent in a special election on January~25, 2022 (Vela replacing Casar), while District~9 changed in the December~13, 2022 runoff (Qadri replacing Tovo, taking office January~6, 2023). These transitions are staggered by nearly 11 months, which undermines the use of a single ``Post-2022'' treatment indicator. Standard electoral turnover does not inherently constitute an institutional shock to zoning policy; the 2022 transitions were distinctive because they represented explicit ideological shifts from preservationist incumbents to pro-housing candidates.

A difference-in-differences specification interacting a ``Flipped District'' indicator with a ``Post-2022'' temporal indicator yields a coefficient of \metricFlipDiDCoeff{} ($p=\metricFlipDiDPval{}$) on petition filing rates. However, formal causal inference is not feasible here: with only two treated districts, staggered treatment timing, a short post-treatment window, and sparse petition events per district, standard cluster-robust inference is known to over-reject \cite{cameron2008bootstrap}. Even wild-bootstrap corrections can fail with very few treated clusters. This estimate should be interpreted as suggestive descriptive evidence only.

\section{Qualitative Planning Context}
\label{sec:interviews}

While the quantitative analysis establishes the predictive boundaries of opposition prediction, the interpretation of these models is clarified by qualitative evidence. Semi-structured interviews with urban planning professionals and city staff (conducted under Columbia University IRB Protocol ACYY0820) surface dynamics that administrative data cannot capture. 

These interviews inform the interpretation of the quantitative findings by detailing the institutional timelines and two distinct motivations within the Austin zoning process:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Declining petition leverage before 2022:} Quantitative models strictly bound the regime shift to the November 2022 supermajority election. Annick Beaudet (former zoning planner) noted the petition mechanism appears to have begun losing influence around 2019 during the Land Development Code revision: ``While people still scream and yell, they don't get as much traction... Necessity is the mother of invention.'' This was corroborated by Alina Carnahan, who pointed to the Hancock neighborhood rezoning as the specific 2022 turning point that solidified the 9-2 pro-housing supermajority. Together, these testimonies confirm that while the 2022 election made the petition mechanism less consequential in practice, the petition had already lost practical force as a veto point long before formal state legislation (HB 24) dismantled it.

    \item \textbf{The Function of Opposition:} Highlighting the nuanced motivations driving public testimony, AURA board member Felicity Maxwell observed: ``We're not talking about suppressing opposition, but understanding it better to have more productive conversations.'' Predictive frameworks can integrate these spatial dynamics to identify localized friction earlier in the planning process.
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric Transparency and Silent Supporters:} Jonathan Tomko (Principal Planner) emphasized that the municipal public comment process lacks any mechanism for \textit{anonymous} public testimony against a zoning case. Because neighborhood social dynamics often penalize residents who deviate from their neighborhood association's preservationist stance, Tomko noted that the formal opposition data is not strictly representative of the entire community. This aligns with Beaudet's observation that ``haters are overrepresented and silent supporters are underrepresented,'' which likely makes the observed opposition data an incomplete measure of community sentiment.
    \item \textbf{Two distinct motivations and geography:} Marla Torrado (Displacement Prevention Division) stressed that opposition mechanisms may be driven by differing underlying concerns. While the ``vocal opposition'' on the affluent West side leverage environmental overlays, East Austin neighborhoods frequently mobilize opposition mechanisms in response to rapid gentrification pressures. This context reinforces the severity of model miscalibration (Layer C1 ECE = \metricECEOODBootstrapCalibrated{}): using rigid probability thresholds could miss communities facing real displacement risk, failing ``to identify where people that are in actual need actually are'' because the algorithm cannot separate affluent NIMBYism from neighborhood preservation efforts.
\end{itemize}

These institutional realities underscore why the predictive model performs best as a relative ranking heuristic rather than a precise probability estimator.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study has several important limitations.

\textbf{Small NLP sample.} The \metricNLPCorpus{} cases used for text-frame classification are small relative to the \metricBaselineCases{}-case analytic sample, raising overfitting concerns for the NLP components. The number of models explored should be viewed as a sensitivity exercise rather than a competitive selection process.

\textbf{Measurement error in reconstructed dates.} Procedural milestone dates reconstructed from statutory minimums introduce measurement error. Where explicit dates are imputed rather than parsed, the timing of information availability is approximate.

\textbf{Selection into observed cases.} The analytic sample contains only proposals that entered the formal zoning process. Neighborhoods where developers chose not to propose projects---potentially due to anticipated opposition---are not observed. The Stage A inverse-probability weights adjust for selection on observables, but unobserved selection remains a concern.

\textbf{Limited post-policy windows.} HB~24 falls entirely outside the observation period. The event-study results should be interpreted as preliminary.

\textbf{Calibration error context.} The opposition model produces a Layer C1 Expected Calibration Error of \metricECEOODBootstrapCalibrated{}. Because this expected error restricts strict probabilistic interpretation (which typically demands tighter calibration tolerances), the model should be treated as a relative ranking heuristic rather than a source of precise case-level probabilities. Regardless, the features generate spatial discrimination (top-decile lift of \metricTopDecileLift{}).

\textbf{Proxy concerns and macro-level aggregation.} This study explicitly excludes individual-level demographic profiling algorithms, such as Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (BISG), from the predictive matrix. Because case-level predictions restrict features to macro-level aggregates (e.g., Census Tract demographics), the model intentionally cannot track highly localized 200ft-neighbor behavioral variations. Although other permitted macro variables proxy for these attributes, integrating exact individual-level demographics remains an area for future work but poses substantive empirical and defense concerns regarding spatial scaling.

\textbf{Fairness power.} The \metricFNRGap{} FNR gap across \metricNDistricts{} council districts should not be interpreted as evidence of equalized error rates. Positive cases per district range from \metricMinDistrictPositives{} to \metricMaxDistrictPositives{} (median: \metricMedianDistrictPositives{}), so the metric lacks statistical power to detect meaningful disparities.

\textbf{Geographic error distribution.} Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_error} maps the geographic distribution of false positive predictions against the locations of actual petition filings. False positives split 49.6\% west of I-35 and 50.4\% east, providing additional spatial context. However, neither this geographic symmetry nor the FNR gap constitutes a formal fairness guarantee. The more substantive ethical limitation is that the model cannot distinguish affluent exclusionary mobilization from anti-displacement resistance (see Section~\ref{sec:interviews}).

\textbf{External validity.} Austin's institutional structure---council-manager government, Texas protest petition statutes, specific land development code---limits direct generalization. The methodological approach, including time-stamped features, sequential evaluation, and calibration benchmarks, is portable, but the specific results are Austin-specific.

\section{Conclusion}

Filing-date administrative features can rank petition risk in Austin's pre-HB\,24 zoning regime, but they do not yet yield portable, well-calibrated probabilities for administrative deployment.

This thesis asks whether measured threshold-crossing petitions against housing development proposals can be predicted before formal public hearings. The answer is qualified: spatial, economic, and site characteristics carry predictive information about petition risk, but the model does not meet the calibration benchmarks required for administrative deployment. Under the primary evaluation---out-of-distribution bootstrap at the filing-date horizon---the model achieves a PR-AUC of \metricBootstrapFiling{} (95\% CI: \metricBootstrapFilingCI{}), with primary-layer calibration error (Layer C1 ECE = \metricECEOODBootstrapCalibrated{}) exceeding the 0.05 threshold. In-distribution cross-validation provides an upper bound PR-AUC of \metricPRAUC{}. A regression discontinuity at the 20\% petition threshold estimates a \metricRDDelay{}-day increase in council processing time, consistent with the institutional significance of the outcome measure. Descriptive evidence from the 2022 electoral transition suggests that petition filing rates declined in districts that changed political representation, though staggered treatment timing and few treated clusters preclude formal causal claims.

These results point to a ceiling on what prediction from public records alone can achieve. Petition filing is partly driven by factors not observable at the time of filing---neighborhood social networks, informal political dynamics, media attention---and the 2022 council regime change altered the relationship between petitions and outcomes. Stakeholder interviews underscore an irreducible ethical limitation: the model cannot distinguish affluent exclusionary mobilization from community-driven anti-displacement resistance, a distinction that administrative data does not encode.

Future work should extend the observation window to capture HB~24 effects \cite{hamilton2024texas}, explore richer text features from pre-hearing public comments, and isolate the exact mechanisms of the 2021--2022 temporary covariate shock.

\subsection*{Synthesis of Longitudinal Findings}

\noindent A comparative analysis of the unclustered (Figure~\ref{fig:unclustered_dynamic}) and clustered (Figure~\ref{fig:clustered_dynamic}) exhibits yields three critical conclusions for the administrative application of urban risk models:

\begin{enumerate}

CatBoost is structurally demographic; LightGBM is structurally spatial. This suggests that a model's ``narrative'' of NIMBYism is an inherent property of its architecture more than its training year.
    \item \textbf{Persistence Through the V-Shaped Recovery:} Even during the predictive recovery of 2024 (where PR-AUC fully rebounded), the models did not converge.     \item \textbf{Auditing for Implicit Bias:} The consistent dominance of \textit{Median Household Income} in the CatBoost clusters demonstrates that without explicit causal penalization (like V-REx), standard gradient boosting will persistently default to socio-economic proxies as their most efficient path to predictive lift, regardless of current urban trends.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodological Appendix: Expanded Details}
\label{app:methodology_details}

\subsection{Variable Dictionary}
This thesis constructs features from multiple municipal and state sources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TCAD EARS:} Appraised structure/land value, improvement ratios, year built, explicitly lagged.
    \item \textbf{Austin Open Data:} Base zoning codes (SF-1 through SF-6, MF-1 through MF-6, commercial variants), overlay districts (NP, NCCD), historic designations (HD), and environmental buffers.
    \item \textbf{ACS 5-Year Interpolations:} Block-group demographics matched to parcel geometry, including median household income, tenure (owner-vs-renter ratios), bachelor's degree attainment, and race/ethnicity vectors, which are explicitly excluded from the predictive policy specification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Search Grids}
Tree-based models, such as CatBoost and Random Forest, utilized the following randomized search bounds across 5-fold temporal cross validation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learning Rate:} Log-uniform [0.01, 0.3]
    \item \textbf{Maximum Tree Depth:} [4, 6, 8, 10]
    \item \textbf{L2 Regularization Base (Leaf Rate):} Uniform [1, 10]
    \item \textbf{Class Imbalance Weighting:} [None, Balanced, SqrtBalanced]
\end{itemize}
Selected CatBoost models uniformly converged on Depth=6, Learning Rate $\approx$ 0.05, and 'Balanced' class weighting.

\subsection{Subgroup Definitions \& Fairness Testing}
To calculate the False Negative Rate (FNR) gaps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geography:} Subgroups defined by the 10 Austin City Council Districts.
    \item \textbf{Socioeconomic Vulnerability:} Subgroups defined by the UT-Austin Uprooted Displacement Risk index (Vulnerable, Active, Chronic).\footnote{Categories follow the 2024 City of Austin update. The original 2018 Uprooted report used a six-category gentrification typology (Susceptible, Early Type 1, Early Type 2, Dynamic, Late, Continued Loss) based on Bates (2013).}
    \item \textbf{Classification Threshold:} To empirically ground the spatial error analysis and prevent misleading impressions caused by severe class imbalance, the binary prediction threshold for FNR evaluation is strictly anchored to the empirical background protest rate ($\mu_{y}$). Any localized zoning project with a predicted opposition probability exceeding the citywide historical baseline is classified as a predicted positive case, ensuring the algorithm's errors are evaluated against actual class incidence rather than arbitrary percentiles.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Natural Language Processing Model: Exploratory Text Features}

Text signals from public hearings post-date the initial filing date prediction horizon and are therefore explored only at later administrative stages. Because these features are unavailable at the time of filing, they are treated as exploratory supplements rather than inputs to the primary opposition model.

The methodology proceeds in three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Transcription.} Audio recordings of City Council and Planning Commission hearings are transcribed locally using Whisper (faster-whisper). Each transcript is linked to its originating zoning case number, producing a corpus of \metricNLPCorpus{} case-level text documents.

    \item \textbf{Seed labeling via large language model.} A randomized sample of \metricNLPSeed{} transcripts is classified by a locally hosted language model (Llama 3.2, 3B parameters). For each transcript, the model is prompted to return two outputs:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Stance:} Whether the overall testimony is supportive, opposed, or neutral toward the proposed zoning change.
        \item \textbf{Argument frames:} Which of five predefined thematic categories appear in the testimony: (1)~traffic and congestion, (2)~infrastructure capacity, (3)~displacement and affordability, (4)~neighborhood character and compatibility, and (5)~procedural fairness. These categories were selected based on recurring themes identified in the urban planning literature on participatory opposition.
    \end{itemize}
    The language model responses are parsed into structured binary labels (frame present or absent) and a three-class stance label. These \metricNLPSeed{} labeled cases serve as the training seed.

    \item \textbf{Supervised expansion across the full corpus.} Using the \metricNLPSeed{} seed labels as training data, six logistic regression classifiers are trained---one for stance and one for each of the five argument frames---with TF-IDF unigram features (1,000-term vocabulary, English stop words removed) as inputs. Each classifier then predicts a continuous probability for every case in the full \metricNLPCorpus{} transcript corpus. The result is a case-level matrix of six predicted probabilities: the likelihood that the hearing testimony is oppositional, and the likelihood that each argument frame is present.
\end{enumerate}

This approach is intentionally lightweight. The seed sample is small, at \metricNLPSeed{} cases, and the classifiers use simple linear models over bag-of-words features. Consequently, the predicted probabilities should be interpreted as coarse thematic indicators rather than precise measurements. The goal is to test whether even rough text signals carry discriminative power for opposition prediction at later horizons, not to build a comprehensive NLP model.

\section{Analysis of Administrative Data Censorship (Stage D)}
\label{app:staged}

\noindent In earlier iterations of this project, a fourth stage (Stage D) attempted to predict the probability that a protested case successfully survived to a final council reading rather than being stalled or withdrawn. However, evaluation of the administrative data pipelines revealed deep structural missingness (censorship): the municipal GIS department rarely finalizes or publishes shapes for cases that are abandoned early in the administrative process. 

A structural diagnostic of the integrated panel confirms this censorship bias. Out of the fully matched cases, nearly \metricAttritionRate{} of protested zoning cases fail to materialize terminal council vote records (an attrition rate that includes cases withdrawn or delayed indefinitely), while a staggering portion of unopposed applications (\metricUnopposedAttritionRate{}) lack full transcript or geometric integration due to never reaching controversial council readings.

Because the underlying datasets systematically censor stalled and abandoned rezonings, predicting ultimate disposition from early-stage administrative features is analytically flawed. The model trivially learns to predict that cases with missing downstream institutional features (such as \texttt{ldb\_source\_year} or \texttt{lui\_matched}) will not receive a final vote. Therefore, we firmly scope the predictive bounds of this thesis to the intermediate threshold: formal petition attainment (Stage~C). Evaluating NIMBY risk strictly on terminal council outcomes systematically underestimates opposition friction.
