Research Interview
The meeting reviewed a research project that combines Austin zoning protest records with parcel‑level tax and city data to model and forecast where residential rezoning opposition has occurred and may occur in the future. Daniel Hardesty Lewis described a multi‑year database of protest letters and related records dating to about 2007, explained that he is training machine‑learning models (with a simple linear regression baseline) to predict protest likelihood by parcel, and noted a Texas law change in September that removed the automatic supermajority trigger for residential rezoning protests, altering the protests’ legal weight. Daniel demonstrated a work‑in‑progress map and discussed calibration problems and data‑filtering bugs discovered in the training set.

Annick Beaudet, a planner with over 25 years’ experience, summarized her career and recent zoning work, noting long‑running neighborhood opposition that stalled missing‑middle and transition zone efforts. She observed that opposition has become more granular—targeting density‑bonus calibration and legal constraints—and argued the protest process is burdensome for systematic planning. Participants discussed practical uses and risks of an AI‑generated opposition map: human intermediaries should introduce and interpret outputs, early one‑on‑one community engagement remains critical, and publishing maps could amplify vocal opposition. The group noted data limitations (minutes and sign‑up sheets skew toward opposition) and agreed to consult retired planning director Greg Guernsey; Annick offered to follow up by email with further input.
