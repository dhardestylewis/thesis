\section*{Summary}

\citet{wu2025bayesian} place a prior over binary selectors $z\in\{0,1\}^p$ indexing the invariant features; the posterior
$p(z\mid\mathcal{D})\propto p(z)\prod_{e,i}\hat{g}(Y^e_i\mid X^z_{ei})/\hat{p}_e(Y^e_i\mid X^z_{ei})$
rewards selectors for which the pooled conditional $\hat{g}$ dominates each local $\hat{p}_e$---a probabilistic lift of Peters et al.'s test.
Consistency (Thm~1) requires $\mu_{\min}>0$; contraction (Thm~2) gives mass on $\{z\neq z^*\}$ decaying as $O(R\,e^{-\kappa nE\mu_{\min}})$.
VI-BIP approximates the posterior by mean-field ELBO maximization, using the U2G estimator to handle discrete gradients; it recovers the same invariant gene sets as exact BIP on a 6{,}170-gene dataset.
\citet{fan2024negdro} take a different tack---a minimax formulation with negative environment weights---returning a point estimate $\hat\beta^\gamma$ with polynomial-time rates but no uncertainty.

\section*{Critique}

The contraction exponent $\kappa nE\mu_{\min}$ is more informative than it looks.
Rearranging the bound to achieve $\epsilon$ posterior mass on bad selectors requires
$nE \gtrsim \frac{p\log 2 + \log(1/\epsilon)}{\kappa\mu_{\min}}$,
i.e.\ roughly $O(p/(\mu_{\min}E))$ samples per environment.
This makes explicit a tension the paper leaves implicit: $\mu_{\min}$ is a property of the \emph{intervention distribution}, not the sample, so collecting more data within a given set of environments hits a wall once $n$ is large but $E$ is fixed.
For my NIMBYism project with $K\approx4$ regimes, the bound says I need $\mu_{\min}$ to be large---meaning the policy shifts (parking reform, HOME Initiative) must genuinely alter the distribution of non-causal features---and that I cannot compensate for weak interventions purely by accumulating more census tracts.

Against NegDRO, the difference is not just uncertainty vs.\ point estimate.
NegDRO's negative weights can \emph{amplify} contrasts between specific environment pairs, effectively up-weighting interventions that most violate invariance.
BIP, by contrast, treats environments symmetrically inside the product likelihood; it cannot selectively stress-test a single environment pair.
This means NegDRO may be more powerful when one environment is known \emph{a priori} to be the most informative intervention, while BIP is preferable when intervention quality is unknown and one wants the data to speak.

VI-BIP's mean-field family $\prod_j\mathrm{Bern}(\pi_j)$ ignores posterior correlation between $z_j$ and $z_k$.
For correlated covariates---say, school quality and income in census tracts---the true posterior likely assigns mass jointly to $\{z_j=z_k=1\}$ or $\{z_j=z_k=0\}$ rather than independently.
Mean-field will spread this mass, biasing each $\pi_j$ toward 0.5 and understating our confidence in either covariate being causal.

\section*{Questions}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item The sample complexity argument above suggests a diagnostic: estimate $\hat\mu_e$ for each environment pair, flag pairs where it is near zero as ``weak interventions,'' and prioritize collecting new environments there rather than more data within existing ones. Has this been explored as a practical acquisition strategy within the BIP framework?
  \item Can the U2G estimator be paired with a structured (e.g.\ Ising) variational family to handle correlated selectors, or does U2G's Gaussian relaxation fundamentally assume independence across $z_j$?
\end{enumerate}
