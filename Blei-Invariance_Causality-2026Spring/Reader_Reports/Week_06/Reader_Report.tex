\section*{Summary}

\citet{wu2025bayesian} recast ICP as sparse Bayesian variable selection.
A binary selector $z \in \{0,1\}^p$ indexes the invariant features; the
posterior is
\[
  p(z \mid \mathcal{D})\;\propto\;
  p(z)\prod_{e,i}
  \frac{\hat{g}(Y^e_i \mid X^z_{ei})}{\hat{p}_e(Y^e_i \mid X^z_{ei})},
\]
\noindent where $\hat{g}$ is the pooled conditional and $\hat{p}_e$ is the
per-environment conditional.
The ratio up-weights selectors $z$ for which the pooled fit matches each
local fit---a continuous, probabilistic version of Peters et al.'s
accept/reject test for invariance.

Two theorems characterize the posterior's behavior.
\textbf{Consistency (Thm~1):} if $\mu_{\min}>0$ (positive KL-divergence
between pooled and local conditionals on at least one non-invariant feature),
the posterior concentrates on the true selector $z^*$ as $n,E\to\infty$.
\textbf{Contraction (Thm~2):} the posterior mass on $\{z \neq z^*\}$ shrinks
as $O\!\left(R\,e^{-\kappa nE\mu_{\min}}\right)$, where $R = 2^p$ is the
hypothesis count, $E$ the number of environments, $n$ the per-environment
sample size, and $\kappa$ a universal constant.
Three practical implications follow.
First, environments and data are interchangeable in the exponent: fewer
environments can be compensated by more data and vice versa, which is
encouraging given that my NIMBYism application has only $K \approx 4$
regimes.
Second, $R = 2^p$ appears only in the polynomial prefactor, not the exponent,
so the curse of dimensionality is milder than it might appear for large $p$.
Third, $\mu_{\min}$ is a KL quantity measuring how much the non-invariant
features actually shift across environments; weakly heterogeneous environments
slow contraction regardless of $n$ or $E$.

\textbf{VI-BIP} approximates $p(z\mid\mathcal{D})$ with a mean-field family
$q(z) = \prod_j \mathrm{Bernoulli}(z_j; \pi_j)$, maximizing the ELBO.
The key computational challenge is that the ELBO gradient requires
differentiating through a discrete draw.
The paper applies the \textbf{U2G estimator}, which relaxes the discrete
$z \sim q$ to a continuous Gaussian surrogate before backpropagating,
yielding low-variance, unbiased gradient estimates compatible with standard
stochastic optimizers.
On a 6{,}170-gene perturbation dataset, VI-BIP recovers the same invariant
gene sets as exact BIP while running orders of magnitude faster.

\section*{Critique \& Contrast with the Optimization Approach}

\citet{fan2024negdro} offer a useful contrast.
They replace the combinatorial posterior with a minimax objective
\[
  \min_{b}\max_{w \in U(\gamma)}
  \textstyle\sum_e w_e\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}[(Y^e - b^\top X^e)^2],
\]
where $U(\gamma)$ permits negative environment weights to enforce risk
parity.
NegDRO returns a point estimate $\hat{\beta}^\gamma$ with explicit
polynomial-time convergence and finite-sample rates, but no uncertainty
quantification.
The key conceptual difference is that BIP produces a \emph{distribution}
over invariant sets, revealing which features are ambiguous when environments
are few, while NegDRO produces a single answer with provable rates but no
sense of the remaining ambiguity.
For my project with $\sim$4 environments and $\sim$50 features, the
multi-modal structure of the BIP posterior is precisely what I need:
it tells me \emph{where} more environments would reduce ambiguity, not
just what the current best estimate is.

\section*{Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The contraction rate depends on $\mu_{\min}$ across environments.
      In practice, one can estimate $\hat\mu_e$ from data, but the bound
      uses the population quantity.
      Is there a data-driven way to certify that $\mu_{\min}$ is bounded
      away from zero before fitting BIP, analogous to the pre-test in
      classical ICP where one first checks whether any invariant set exists?
    \item VI-BIP's mean-field approximation factorizes across features,
      which ignores posterior correlations between $z_j$ and $z_k$ for
      correlated covariates.
      In the NIMBYism setting, regulatory features (zoning category,
      parking requirements) are strongly correlated.
      Does the mean-field gap systematically bias $\pi_j$ toward 0.5 for
      correlated features, and if so, would a structured (e.g.\
      Ising-model) variational family recover sharper posteriors?
\end{enumerate}
