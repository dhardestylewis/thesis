\section*{Summary: Revisiting VI-BIP and the Core ICP Theorems}

\citet{wu2025bayesian} frame ICP as sparse Bayesian variable selection.
The binary selector $z \in \{0,1\}^p$ marks invariant features; the
posterior
\[
  p(z \mid \mathcal{D})\;\propto\;
  p(z)\prod_{e,i}
  \frac{\hat{g}(Y^e_i \mid X^z_{ei})}{\hat{p}_e(Y^e_i \mid X^z_{ei})}
\]
up-weights $z$ whose pooled conditional $\hat{g}$ dominates each local
conditional $\hat{p}_e$---a likelihood-ratio test embedded in a prior.
\textbf{Consistency (Thm~1)} requires only $\mu_{\min}>0$ (positive
KL-divergence between pooled and local conditionals on at least one bad
feature); the posterior then concentrates on $z^*$.
\textbf{Contraction (Thm~2)} sharpens this: the posterior mass on
$\{z: z\neq z^*\}$ decays as $O(R\,e^{-\kappa n E \mu_{\min}})$,
where $R=2^p$ is the model count, $E$ the number of environments, and
$\kappa$ a universal constant.
Two messages emerge: (i) the exponential in $nE\mu_{\min}$ means
\emph{either} more data or more diverse environments can substitute for
each other; (ii) because $R=2^p$ appears only in the pre-exponential,
large feature spaces slow convergence polynomially in $p$, not
exponentially---a surprisingly benign curse of dimensionality.

\textbf{VI-BIP} approximates $p(z\mid\mathcal{D})$ via a mean-field
family $q(z)=\prod_j q_j(z_j)$, optimizing ELBO.
The obstacle is that the ELBO gradient involves discrete $z$; the paper
applies the \textbf{U2G estimator} (unbiased-to-Gaussian), which
reparameterizes the discrete draw through a continuous Gaussian relaxation
before taking gradients.
This allows standard stochastic gradient ascent despite the combinatorial
support.
The approximation cost is a gap between the true posterior and $q^*$;
in practice, on a 6{,}170-gene perturbation dataset, VI-BIP recovers
the same invariant genes as exact BIP while being orders of magnitude faster.

\citet{fan2024negdro} offer a complementary perspective: recast ICP as a\
continuous minimax problem
\[
  \min_{b}\max_{w \in U(\gamma)}
  \textstyle\sum_e w_e\,\hat{\mathbb{E}}[(Y^e - b^\top X^e)^2],
\]
where $U(\gamma)$ admits negative weights.
By contrast with BIP's combinatorial posterior, NegDRO has a convex inner
problem and differentiable outer problem, enabling polynomial-time
convergence.
The conceptual gap is that BIP produces a \emph{distribution} over
invariant sets---revealing ambiguity when environments are few---while
NegDRO returns a single point estimate $\hat\beta^\gamma$ with explicit
finite-sample rates but no uncertainty quantification.

\section*{Causal Representation Learning (Schölkopf et al.)}

\citet{scholkopf2021toward} argue that robustness and out-of-distribution
generalization require not merely statistical patterns but an
\emph{approximate causal model} of the data-generating process.
Their central organizing principle is the \textbf{independent causal
mechanisms (ICM)} conjecture: the causal Markov factorization
$p(X_1,\ldots,X_d)=\prod_i p(X_i\mid \mathrm{pa}_i)$ decomposes reality
into modules that are \emph{independently} modifiable by interventions.
Anti-causal directions---modeling effects given causes---violate ICM
because they conflate mechanism with marginal, yielding fragile predictors
that break under distributional shift.
This connects directly to ICP: Peters et al.'s invariant set $S^*$ is
precisely the set whose \emph{causal} conditional $p(Y\mid X_{S^*})$ is
stable, while anti-causal features' conditionals shift.

The paper surveys three operationalizations of ICM: (1) independence of
noise variables in additive SEMs (link to ICP); (2) Kolmogorov-complexity
arguments implying that $p(\text{cause})$ and $p(\text{effect}\mid
\text{cause})$ compress independently; (3) disentangled representation
learning, where latent variables recover causal factors.
While the survey is technically rich, it does not yet deliver a single
unified algorithm---the ``messiness'' Blei notes is real: the paper is a
call to arms rather than a solved problem.

\section*{Critique \& Project Connection}

For my \textit{Predicting NIMBYism Causally} project the Schölkopf
framework clarifies a modeling choice I had left implicit.
My protest indicator $Y$ (zoning opposition) is an \emph{effect} of
regulatory and demographic causes; I have been modeling $p(Y\mid X)$,
an anti-causal direction, which ICM theory warns will be fragile across
the policy regimes I use as environments.
The appropriate posture is to encode the regulatory shifts as
\emph{interventions} on the causal graph and identify $S^*$ as the
features whose \emph{causal} conditional is intervention-stable---precisely
what ICP formalizes.
VI-BIP's posterior is especially useful here: with only four temporal
environments and $\sim50$ features, the posterior over $z$ will be
multi-modal, and those multi-modal regions identify the features where
additional environment diversity is most needed.
NegDRO's fast point estimate can serve as a computational screen before
committing to the full VI-BIP run.

\section*{Questions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The contraction rate $O(R\,e^{-\kappa nE\mu_{\min}})$ treats all
      $R=2^p$ candidate sets equally in the pre-factor.
      In practice, sparsity priors (e.g.\ Beta-Bernoulli with small mean)
      reduce the effective model count.
      Does the contraction result sharpen when $p(z)$ concentrates on sparse
      $z$, or does the $2^p$ bound remain tight regardless of the prior?
    \item Schölkopf et al.\ argue that anti-causal predictors are less
      robust, yet NegDRO optimizes a squared-loss objective on
      $p(Y\mid X)$---ostensibly anti-causal.
      NegDRO's invariance guarantee holds under additive interventions
      on $X$; does this suffice to recover causal features even when the
      regression direction is anti-causal, or does an anti-causal
      orientation introduce a systematic bias that its finite-sample rates
      do not capture?
\end{enumerate}
